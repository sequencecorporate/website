Artificial intelligence

Are we looking at AI through the right lens?

Three practical ways to challenge our thinking and be part of the AI solution.

Kevin Maguire · 26 October 2026 · Business & change · 5 min read

Three things worth considering

  1. Help someone else take a step forward
  2. Turn the next negative AI story into a learning opportunity
  3. Try changing how you work with AI

The world of AI leaves us all with a lot to ponder every day.

I often reflect that we've already become desensitised to the term “AI” or “Artificial Intelligence”. It's become an everyday expression, increasingly polarised and almost a vague concept, just when we should be digging deeper and learning more about what it can and cannot do.

Many of our conversations about AI seem to contain an unhealthy level of cynicism. In some respects, I think we want to dislike AI because it is starting to replace human cognition and thinking in so many areas.

One minute I hear people saying AI just produces “rubbish”, and the next that it's going to take over the world and kill us all. I struggle to think both of those things can be true? Of course, today's limitations and tomorrow's risks aren't necessarily contradictory, but I do wonder whether we're giving either argument enough thought.

So where does that leave us?

Well, as with all 'difficult' subjects, I think we need to try harder.

We don't normally leave human team members unsupervised, so why do we so often talk about AI doing things it shouldn't, as though the technology itself must be the problem?

Yes, introducing guardrails, giving longer and more comprehensive instructions and checking the output all take time. Sometimes it feels like we're doing the very work AI was supposed to take off our hands.

But I think this reveals a fundamental difference in how people see AI.

We should be using AI to leverage (i.e. multiply) our effort, not simply reduce or simplify it.

Firstly, why wouldn't we? It allows us to continue applying human knowledge, experience and judgement while drawing on capabilities that can substantially extend what we're able to achieve. That sounds like a win-win to me.

And secondly, didn't we just worry that AI might take over the world? Well, it will if we don't work with it. Surely understanding it, maintaining our own capabilities and playing an active part in how it develops is a better response than simply standing back.

When human team members produce material we think is poor, we don't call it “human slop”. We intervene, give feedback and try to improve the outcome.

So why do we so often talk about “AI slop” without the “so therefore we need to…” follow-on?

Of course, AI isn't a human colleague, and there are important differences in how it works and learns. But the principle of taking responsibility for the quality of what we ask for, how we supervise it and what we do with the result still applies.

We should embrace the capability while stepping up our own input and responsibility. The quality of instructions, boundaries and supervision should reflect both the capability of the system and the consequences of getting something wrong, rather than AI simply being left to its own devices.

Many of us grew up with technology expressions like “garbage in, garbage out”, but we often seem to forget this when talking about AI. We shouldn't.

As a species, we need to think more about our own role in getting useful results and continuing to learn new technology, rather than just letting it happen to us.

Do you remember conversations some years ago along the lines of:

“You booked a holiday online? What, without calling anyone?”

Where one looked at the other like they are a weirdo.

I suspect we'll have similar conversations again with AI. But the technology becoming commonplace doesn't mean we'll automatically become good at using it.

That part is down to us.

Three things worth considering

A note for all three

Only share confidential or personal information through an AI service approved for that purpose—for example, a workplace system with appropriate privacy and security safeguards.

01

Help someone else take a step forward

Can you spend 30 minutes with a friend, family member or colleague helping them understand something about AI, improve how they use it or become more confident experimenting with it?

There are lots of ways to take a step forward, depending on where they're starting. For example:

  • Move beyond the AI summary in Google search and try a multi-stage conversation with an LLM (e.g. ChatGPT, Claude etc.). Ask it to explain something unfamiliar, challenge its explanation, request an example and then explore something you still don't understand.
  • Create a project with your own documents and background information to give the LLM more context and see how that improves its responses.
  • Explore a desktop AI application and how it can help work with files and software on your computer, where the application supports those capabilities and you've authorised access.
  • Experiment with AI agents that can carry out multi-step tasks, perhaps starting with something straightforward such as gathering information or producing a recurring summary.

Teaching someone else can also expose gaps in your own understanding and help you develop your skills along the way.

You cannot cover all of these in 30 minutes. Just help someone take their next step, or next conversation.

02

Turn the next negative AI story into a learning opportunity

The next time you hear a story about AI getting something wrong, ask yourself what actually went wrong.

Was it the technology, the instructions, the information supplied, the supervision or the way the output was used?

And consider the circumstances. Was this an unexpected failure in a live environment, or something discovered during deliberate testing? Companies developing AI systems routinely experiment, test boundaries and investigate failures precisely to understand and improve their technology. A troubling result uncovered during testing isn't necessarily evidence of an uncontrolled real-world incident.

That doesn't mean dismissing genuine failures or risks. It means understanding what happened before drawing conclusions.

Is there something you could learn from the story to make your own use of AI more effective or safer?

03

Try changing how you work with AI

Next time you have something you want AI to help with, try asking it to write the prompt for you first — don't just rush in with your question. Explain what you're trying to achieve and ask what information it needs to create a really good prompt.

Better still, try using your microphone. Talk through what's on your mind, including the background, uncertainties and half-formed ideas you might not bother typing. See whether providing that richer context changes the quality of the response. Perhaps combine this with the prompt-generation idea above.

You might be surprised how much difference a change in approach can make.
