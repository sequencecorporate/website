When “computer says no” becomes a risk

Misplaced compliance reflexes can be just as dangerous as compliance ignored.

Acting as executor of an estate, I recently contacted an insurer about an existing pet insurance policy. Ongoing cover was still required, so I wanted to understand the terms before deciding how to proceed.

The exchange was revealing:

Insurer: “Unfortunately, owing to General Data Protection Regulation Laws, I am only able to discuss any policy details until the Transfer of Interest Form is completed.”

Me: “Unfortunately, the Transfer of Interest Form cannot be completed based on the information available as it contains the following statement ‘I confirm that I am happy to accept sole responsibility for the above-mentioned policy and adhere to the policy terms and conditions.’ Please provide the policy details and all terms and conditions so that this confirmation can be given…”

Insurer: “I do apologise that we have been unable to discuss the policy info, details of a policy can only be provided with a transfer of interest completed or with proof of power of attorney.”

In other words, I was being asked to accept responsibility for an insurance policy without seeing its terms or knowing the premium or cancellation options.

The explanation? Data protection. Or, in their language, “General Data Protection Regulation Laws” (some readers will already be cringing).

There was an interesting inconsistency. The insurer appeared willing to accept a power of attorney, which ordinarily ceases to confer authority on death, as sufficient comfort to discuss the policy. Yet it was unwilling to consider providing generic policy information without disclosing personal details or requiring me to assume contractual obligations.

It seemed prepared to tolerate legal uncertainty within its established process, but not to consider an alternative that might have presented considerably less risk.

This was also an FCA-regulated insurer, operating in an environment where customers ordinarily receive substantial information about the cover they are purchasing. I was not suggesting that identical requirements necessarily applied to a transfer following death. Nevertheless, asking an executor to accept unknown obligations without even seeing the policy terms or premium should surely have prompted someone to reconsider the process, especially when politely invited to do so on the grounds that it made little legal sense.

Data protection had become the reflex. Other considerations appeared to have fallen out of view.

When the controls become the risk

I have spent much of my career on the other side of these decisions. Designing controls involves balancing legal and regulatory requirements, operational practicality and the need for employees to apply them consistently. There is rarely a perfect answer.

Large organisations, in particular, need standardised processes that can be followed safely without requiring everyone on the front line to exercise specialist judgement. Some outcomes will inconvenience customers, and sometimes that is unavoidable and justified.

An organisation should not have to apologise for controls that are necessary to protect it. It should, however, understand the consequences of applying them and be prepared to acknowledge when they create an unintended or dysfunctional outcome.

The danger is that individually defensible controls can interact to produce outcomes nobody intended. Data protection, contractual obligations, the UK Listing Rules, sector-specific regulation and operational controls may each have been considered carefully in isolation. Their combined effect may tell a different story.

Who owns the outcome when individually sound controls produce a collectively unsound result?

The cultural problem: escalation and organisational learning

Employees should not be expected to improvise exceptions or interpret complex legal obligations. Managers may be entirely right to uphold an established procedure, even when it creates an inconvenient outcome.

However, there is a difference between following a process and assuming that following it must produce the right answer.

Consider a customer who challenges a restriction and receives the standard explanation. They then raise a specific objection to that explanation and ask for the position to be reconsidered. The employee refers the matter to a manager, only to return with precisely the original explanation.

The problem is not necessarily that the decision remained unchanged. It is that the second answer failed to address the second question.

The customer asked why. The organisation answered what, and might as well have said, “computer says no”.

Repeated often enough, this approach risks teaching employees that noticing problems is outside their responsibilities.

We have probably all heard someone say, "I can see this heading for a major problem. I've tried to tell them, but they don't listen."

That should concern any organisation.

You may have reduced the risk of an employee making the wrong decision, while increasing the risk that nobody tells you when the organisation is making one.

Many employees care about their work, their customers and their organisation's reputation. A good control environment should preserve that commitment, not discourage it.

Organisations already have whistleblowing arrangements, complaints procedures and suggestion schemes. Perhaps there is also a case for a simple, recognised route for constructive challenge: somewhere to flag an unintended consequence without alleging wrongdoing or asking for an exception.

It need not be another department or bureaucracy. The important thing is that an observation reaches someone able to decide whether it deserves attention, even if the immediate decision cannot change.

Otherwise, a process can become self-validating.

A customer raises a complaint. The organisation checks that its procedure was followed. The complaint is closed and recorded as resolved. Success? It certainly is for the statistics department.

From the organisation's perspective, the system has worked. Yet the customer's substantive challenge may never have been examined. The same problem remains for the next customer, along with the potential legal or financial risk that the customer was trying to bring to the organisation's attention.

Following the process is evidence of compliance with the process. It is not, by itself, evidence that the process is right.

The opposite reflex

There is an equivalent danger at the other end of the spectrum.

In smaller businesses, "we need to stay nimble" can become as automatic a response as "computer says no" in a large organisation.

Informality and entrepreneurial judgement are valuable. They allow businesses to respond quickly and avoid disproportionate bureaucracy.

They should not, however, become reasons to overlook controls that are genuinely necessary.

The challenge is not to maximise or minimise process. It is to recognise that well-designed controls can protect your money, not simply consume it. Avoiding the cost of preventable mistakes, disputes, regulatory fines, unnecessary extra legal advice and decisions that nobody thought to document is a valuable asset to any business, especially one that prides itself on being “agile and entrepreneurial”.

Three things worth considering

01

For large and complex organisations — test a case that passed.

Select a completed customer case or transaction involving several control functions that was recorded as satisfactorily handled. Reconstruct the outcome with the relevant control owners. Did compliance with one requirement create risks under another, or an outcome nobody intended? Would your assurance framework have identified those risks, or did it simply confirm that each control was followed?

02

For medium-sized and growing businesses — try the second-question test.

Review a recent customer challenge that was escalated. Did the manager address the specific question raised after the original response, or simply confirm the existing procedure? Consider requiring escalations to identify the point that remains unanswered. If the original decision stands but the concern appears legitimate, is there a route for reviewing the underlying process?

03

For smaller and entrepreneurial businesses — challenge one decision not to have a process.

Identify a recurring activity managed informally because the business values speed and flexibility. What happens if the person who normally handles it is absent, a decision is disputed or transaction volumes double? Would a simple checklist, approval threshold or written record prevent avoidable costs without sacrificing agility?

Good compliance requires people to follow the rules. Great compliance needs people who care whether the rules are working, and organisations willing to improve, adapt or strengthen them.
